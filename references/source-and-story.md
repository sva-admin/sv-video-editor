# Source evidence and story structure

## Map what exists

Start from the existing inventory. An archive listing is not extracted media; a remote listing is not a local file. Request missing selections by asset ID, purpose, approximate usable range and size. Download or proxy the needed ranges before recommending a full-library copy.

For each source, retain:

- A stable asset ID, original location, byte size and content hash.
- Original versus derivative, and the derivative's source hash and settings.
- Duration, source frame rate and time base, orientation, pixel format, color metadata and audio streams.
- Observed content, known context, rights or identity restrictions and uncertainty.
- Coverage: metadata only, sampled times, watched intervals, transcript model and listened intervals.

Keep stable IDs independent of filenames. Relocation changes the locator, not the asset's identity. A changed source hash invalidates cached derivatives. Collisions in shortened hashes need explicit handling.

Build a small review packet first: the decision, source IDs, exact excerpts, a labelled contact sheet, relevant crops and unknowns. Keep full extraction on disk. Give collaborators this packet instead of repeatedly decoding the same library. When an image is too small to verify a name or number, inspect its original crop.

An inventory graph can show category counts and measured durations, but label the basis. Probed duration is not time watched. Duplicates and proxies are not additional unique footage. Thumbnails need actual source provenance and representative content. Do not invent missing category hours.

## Define a truthful narrative

Write four sentences before choosing transitions:

1. This audience needs to understand or decide something specific.
2. The supplied material can demonstrate these claims.
3. The film moves from this starting question through these linked actions to this result or current stage.
4. The closing asks for this plausible next step.

A corporate film may connect context, capability, a concrete case, the team's method and an invitation. Use only the chapters the evidence supports. A chronological story, a direct introduction or a question-led opening can each work. Choose based on audience, viewing situation and usable material, not a universal hook rule.

When comparing two directions, show both fairly: opening words, first images, when the organisation appears, chapter order, what changes and why. Preserve the original outline's purpose. Use labels such as supplied outline and proposed sequence until actual versions exist. A proposed line from a website is not an on-camera quote unless that recording exists and is approved.

## Build connections, not a montage of unrelated clips

For each beat, note its message, evidence, image, audio, emotional pace and link to the next beat. Useful connections include:

| Connection | Use when | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Need to action | A person explains a problem and a real task responds | The action belongs to the stated project or is clearly contextual |
| Action to consequence | Work leads to a visible result or next step | The evidence establishes the relationship |
| Wide to detail | A new setting needs orientation before close work | Location, people and time remain coherent |
| Question to answer | Speech or a business concern sets up the following evidence | The film eventually answers the question |
| Shared movement or sound | A transition connects different places | Style does not imply a false event or continuity |
| Relationship to contribution | An event or partnership precedes a case | State the precise role rather than suggesting ownership |

Keep a claim ledger: claim, source, exact support, attribution, uncertainty and approval. Separate signed agreement, work in progress, completed work and measured outcome. Attractive stock or generated imagery can illustrate a concept with suitable disclosure; it cannot prove real capacity, relationships or achievements.

## Select speech without changing meaning

Listen around the selected sentence, including its lead-in and continuation. Keep complete thoughts, the final word and particles that carry meaning. Remove repetition only if the remaining statement is faithful. Never splice fragments into a claim the speaker did not make.

Maintain separate fields for exact recorded speech, proposed narration, pronunciation text, display text and translations. Scratch pronunciation substitutions must not leak into captions. Verify names, titles and number readings with an approved terminology sheet. Ask about ambiguous calendar systems instead of automatically converting years.

For real interviews, subtitle the actual speech. For translated subtitles, preserve its meaning and identify the reviewer. ASR is a search and alignment aid; verify selected lines by listening. When the mouth is visible, picture and sound must come from the matching moment.

## Reuse the story across deliverables

Short versions need their own beginning, evidence and ending. A vertical crop needs its own safe composition and caption review. Neither is automatically the first minute of the master.

An accompanying presentation supports a conversation. Sequence it around the audience's questions: what the organisation does, whom it serves, evidence of capability, a selected case, how work begins and contact. Reuse approved claims and media, but do not dump every film chapter onto a slide. Keep editable deliverables in the formats promised.
